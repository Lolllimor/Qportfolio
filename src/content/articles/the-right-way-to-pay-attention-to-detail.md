At a point when I was a kid, I had this urge to make my mom happy. I think it started after I went to the boarding house. I knew what was right, and I did not want her to have anything avoidable to complain about.

But there was a problem. She still complained.

When she got back from work, I would welcome her and tell her the things I had done around the house. She would thank me, and many times do it over anyway. Sometimes I would catch her ranting under her breath as she redid them, and the next day I would try harder — use more water to wash the dishes, sweep more than once. But I just wasn’t doing it well enough.

The problem wasn’t that my mom is hard to please. She really isn’t. The problem was that I wasn’t paying attention to details.

## What “attention to detail” actually means

“Pay attention to the details” — I read it, I heard it in courses, I heard it from adults and teachers. But what exactly is that? If you already know what it means, it’s easy to laugh off. But I wish someone had said it a bit simpler back then.

My mom appreciated my effort, but there were certain things that mattered, and I wasn’t getting them right. You know the 80/20 rule — where 20% of your effort determines 80% of the result, and vice versa? Attention to detail is something like that.

What made a plate clean?

## The plate that was never really clean

As kids, my siblings and I ate off plastic plates, and oil is a constant in Nigerian food. We washed with bar soap. The problem was I was giving the same attention to the oily plastic plate as I was to everything else on the rack. And bar soap didn’t cut grease all that well to begin with, so I’d dip the not-quite-washed oily plates into the same rinsing water as the other plates and cups, and end up with a bunch of clean-looking but slippery plates.

Paying attention to detail requires you to pay attention to the things that matter. And that principle stretches into every corner of life. The real question is: what are the things that matter?

## In design, the same rule applies

In design, you have a higher chance of getting results that matter when you pay attention to the foundational things — not the flashy things.

Things like consistency, typography, spacing.

These are the clean water of design. You can have a beautiful color palette, a nice illustration, a clever micro-interaction — but if your typography is inconsistent (different weights doing the same job, sizes that don’t follow a scale, line heights that feel arbitrary), the whole thing feels off. And most times the user can’t even tell you why. They just feel like something isn’t right, the same way my mom could tell a plate wasn’t properly washed without needing to explain the chemistry of grease and bar soap.

Consistency works the same way. A button that behaves one way on one screen and slightly differently on another. Spacing that’s 16px here and 14px there for no reason anyone can point to. These aren’t the things people will call out and say “I don’t like this design.” But they’re the things that make a design feel unfinished, or amateur, even when every screen looks fine on its own.

## What the foundations actually look like

It’s one thing to say “pay attention to the foundations.” It’s another to know which foundations, and what to actually do about them. So here’s what that looks like in practice, the same way “wash the oily plate properly before it touches the rinsing water” is more useful than “be clean.”

**Typography.** One of the most common typography errors is using too many size variants in a single design, paired with multiple typefaces. It’s usually best to pick one or two typefaces, and settle on a small set of sizes — big text (64), medium text (32), small text (12) — used consistently across the design. This also does a lot for contrast: it’s easier for a message to stand out when it isn’t competing with five other sizes for attention. And while we’re on it — watch your spelling. It’s a small thing that quietly undercuts everything else you got right.

**Spacing.** Spacing is a small rule that can make a major difference, and it’s really about hierarchy. Spacing helps users understand relationships between elements, so the goal isn’t equal spacing across your design — it’s bringing related elements closer together, and putting more distance between elements that aren’t as related. Get this right and a layout explains itself before the user reads a word of it.

**Consistency.** Before you send a design out to a stakeholder, scan your own design the way a stranger would — not for whether each screen looks good on its own, but for whether the design is actually doing its job. For marketing material: is the message clear? For UI: can the user find what they need, without effort? Inconsistency is rarely one big mistake; it’s a hundred small unrelated decisions made at different times, none of which felt wrong in isolation.

None of this is exciting. That’s the point. The plate isn’t clean because of the smell you added to the soap — it’s clean because the grease actually came off before the rinse. Foundations work the same way: invisible when done right, and the first thing people feel when they’re not.

## More effort isn’t the same as more attention

Using more water didn’t make my plates cleaner. In the same way, doing more doesn’t automatically mean doing better. More screens, more vector elements, more states, more polish on the parts that already work — none of that fixes a foundation that’s shaky.

You can pour all the effort in the world into a design, but if it’s going into the wrong places, you end up with something that clearly took a lot of work — and still isn’t right.

## It starts with intention

Paying attention to detail also starts with intention. Having the intention to do great work keeps the relevant question alive in your mind throughout the process. It’s what lets you notice the things that matter when they matter, not after.

That’s the real difference between me washing plates and my mom washing plates back then. She wasn’t just washing to get through the pile — she had already decided what “clean” meant before she picked up the plate, so she caught the grease while it was still there to deal with. I was just going through the motions, and I only found out something was wrong after the plates were already stacked and slippery.
