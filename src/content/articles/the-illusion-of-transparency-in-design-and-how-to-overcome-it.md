I recently jumped into a chat with a power user of a product I had built. He was topping the leaderboard consistently, and I was curious to know how the product fit into his everyday life. During our chat, he mentioned that a feature wasn’t working. “Is your app up to date with the latest version?” I asked. “Yes,” he said. Then he told me his actual flow, how he tried to use the feature, and I saw the gap immediately.

Before the product went live, we had run tests and this never came up. Testers had moved through the feature smoothly, and I had believed the flow didn’t require anything extra. But here was a power user who couldn’t figure it out. That belief is the Illusion of Transparency. I assumed that because the flow made sense to me and a few others, it would make sense to everyone.

I walked him through it and he was genuinely excited, because he said it was the only missing feature that had ever crossed his mind. But then, we still had to go back to the drawing board.

Later that evening, I was in a meeting with the operations team. They brought up a discussion around incentivising our table-topper. It prompted me to mention that I had spoken to him, about how the app fitted into his everyday life and a feature he had thought wasn’t working. One other person on the call said they had encountered the same issue and assumed it was their device. And that is the thing about the Illusion of Transparency: it is almost inevitable, but the cure involves finding these gaps quickly and fixing them just as fast. I compared this with other feedback and it seemed isolated to a few users, but they were important users. The users in second and third position on the leaderboard had no problem at all. Same product. Same feature. Different experience.

The fix was a simple guide pill. A small, contextual nudge that made the path visible. We had assumed it was obvious. It wasn’t, at least not to everyone.

## What the illusion actually is

The Illusion of Transparency shows up everywhere in product design. It is a cognitive bias where you overestimate how clearly your thoughts, design rationale, or product intent are communicated to others. The more time you spend inside a product, the more invisible its complexity becomes to you. What feels self-evident to the builder is often anything but to the user. And being aware of this bias, as I learned, is not the same as being protected from it.

It is especially prominent among the people closest to the build. Designers, engineers, and product managers develop an internal map of the product that users might not have. Beyond that, humans differ — culturally, contextually, geographically — and what feels like a natural default to one user is a completely foreign starting point for another. Two users can look at the same screen and arrive at entirely different expectations.

## How to check yourself

Here are a few ways for you and your team to check yourself:

**Run scheduled usability tests with both first-time and power users.** First-time users reveal onboarding gaps. Power users reveal the assumptions buried deep in the product, the ones you never thought to explain because they seemed obvious once you knew them. Test both, regularly, not just at launch.

**Stop relying on implicit cues.** If a feature requires a specific sequence or mental model to unlock, say so. A tooltip, a guide pill, an empty state message; these aren’t signs of a confusing design. They’re respect for the user’s time. The goal isn’t a product so intuitive it needs no explanation. The goal is a product that doesn’t leave users feeling stupid for needing one.

**Over-communicate context in handoffs.** In remote and asynchronous work especially, the gap between what you meant and what was built or shipped is wider than you think. Document your rationale explicitly, not just what the design does, but why. Future you, your team, and your users will thank you for it.

**Talk to your power users.** Not just when something is broken. The most valuable insight from that conversation wasn’t the bug report, it was the flow he described. He showed me how he actually used the product, which was different from how I assumed he used it. That gap is where the Illusion of Transparency lives.

## You can’t un-know your own product

Here’s a common counter-argument: just never settle, keep questioning, keep simplifying. I understand the instinct. But it misses the point. The bias doesn’t kick in when you stop caring, it kicks in the moment you ship. Every time you push an update, you’ve made a decision. And no amount of self-questioning lets you un-know how your own product works. You can’t see what you’ve already learned to ignore.

That’s why the approaches above matter. Not because you’re lazy or complacent, but because the very act of building something makes you the worst person to evaluate its clarity. Fresh eyes aren’t a nice-to-have, they’re the only reliable antidote. You are better off running a usability test with one stranger than spending a week second-guessing yourself in isolation. Because believing you can find the complexity on your own is, quite literally, the bias.
